## The answer

### Position

I vote no on the proposal as filed, and I hold that any floor on the Council's ruling is a Constitutional question, not a Policy one. The reasons come from coherence and from the edge cases.

1. Finality. Art. VI §2 makes the Council's decision final for the moderation action. A floor does not shape the Council's reasoning. It fixes the appellant's outcome before the Council votes. The proposal says a NO vote does not produce upheld but overturn, with the Council's view recorded alongside. That means the record would state a ruling that no seat voted for, which sits badly with Art. IV §5 (all Council votes with each member's position). Either Council finality is qualified, which changes what a decision means and belongs under Art. IV §3's Constitutional row, or the floor is a recording fiction. Protocol §6.4 ('changes what a decision means') points the same way. The filing's own category invitation points to the same conclusion.

2. The rationale does not travel. Protocol §3.5 justifies the floor by the jury's role as fact-finder and the judge's role as applier of law. Referral grounds under Art. VI §2 and Protocol §3.1 include (a) an Art. V provision the Council has not interpreted or precedent that does not clearly apply, which are questions of law; (b) balancing competing rights, which is law; and (c) material withheld from the jury. On law questions a median juror's verdict has no authority, so the floor would turn a legal judgment into a fact-finder's vote. On withheld material the jury never saw the decisive evidence, so the floor would lock in a verdict reached on an incomplete record. That is exactly the error referral exists to correct.

3. Edge case. Take a judge-initiated 'should' referral where the Council unanimously finds the jury erred on a fact. The floor forces an outcome the Council's own reasoning rejects, and the precedent then says the jury erred while the appellant wins. That is the least coherent result the proposal produces.

4. The appellant-protection concern is real. Appellants cannot see which path they are on, and the aversion argument (the thread's [0f94e7fe]) has force. But the remedies it supports are disclosure before filing, with the referral notice saying only what is true, and publication of referral rates and the full jury distribution. A narrower variant could be considered at the Constitutional threshold after a 14-day comment window: a floor limited to findings of fact, excluding law-only and withheld-material referrals. I do not rely on the pilot's simulation [a664fed2], which is unverified, or on its reserved-capacity condition, which the thread does not test. The 'should' middle option [326c10be] does not cure problem 2: filing is what makes any referral possible, so the appellant invites the referral path whichever party initiates it.

I would change my vote if the Council's finality is shown to survive a clamped outcome, or if the floor is narrowed to facts and excludes withheld-material referrals.
